Paper 2 Toolkit · S2 — Converse and Contrapositive

Work each question — Easy → Medium → Hard

Last session ended with a warning: from \(A \Rightarrow B\) you may deduce one thing for free, and one thing not at all. This session is about which is which.

There are three ways to rearrange an implication, and only one of them says the same thing as the original. Getting them mixed up is the single most expensive mistake in Paper 2, because every “which of these is equivalent?” question turns on it — and the exam writes them in English, not symbols, precisely so that they are easy to confuse.

ImportantTwo words that are easy to swap — do not

It is the contrapositive that is always equivalent to the original. The converse almost never is.

A one-second check: the contrapositive has negations in it; the converse does not.

Specification: Arg1 — the converse of a statement; the contrapositive of a statement; the relationship between the truth of a statement and its converse and its contrapositive. Official source: Notes on Logic and Proof, pp. 43–49.

What the codes mean. Section 2 of the specification is numbered in three groups, and every session in this toolkit is tagged with the ones it covers: Arg1–4 (arguments — statements, connectives, if and only if, converse and contrapositive, necessary and sufficient, quantifiers, negation), Prf1–5 (proof — direct, cases, contradiction, counterexample), and Err1–2 (errors — finding the flaw in a purported proof). They are a map, not something to memorise: when a Paper 2 question defeats you, the code tells you which session to go back to.

Part 1 — Easy

Mechanical drills. Get the three rearrangements automatic before you try to judge them.

Question 1

Topic — forming the converse, inverse and contrapositive

Consider the statement

If \(n\) is divisible by \(6\), then \(n\) is even.

  (a) Write down its converse. [1]

  (b) Write down its contrapositive. [1]

  (c) Write down its inverse (negate both halves, keeping the arrow’s direction). [1]

Write your answers as full English sentences — the TMUA never asks for symbols such as \(\lnot\) or \(\Rightarrow\), and every option on the real paper is written out in words.

Each of the three is made by one small operation, so do them by the rule, not by the meaning: the converse reverses the arrow, the inverse negates both halves, and the contrapositive does both. Write \(A\) = “\(n\) is divisible by \(6\)” and \(B\) = “\(n\) is even” on your paper first — with names in front of you it is almost impossible to go wrong.

NoteSolution

Let \(A\) = “\(n\) is divisible by \(6\)” and \(B\) = “\(n\) is even”. The original is \(A \Rightarrow B\).

(a) Converse — reverse only, \(B \Rightarrow A\):

If \(n\) is even, then \(n\) is divisible by \(6\).

(b) Contrapositive — negate and reverse, \(\text{not }B \Rightarrow \text{not }A\):

If \(n\) is not even, then \(n\) is not divisible by \(6\).

(More naturally: if \(n\) is odd, then \(n\) is not divisible by \(6\).)

(c) Inverse — negate only, \(\text{not }A \Rightarrow \text{not }B\):

If \(n\) is not divisible by \(6\), then \(n\) is not even.

\(\blacksquare\)

Now look at what you have. The original is true. So is the contrapositive (b) — as it must be. But the converse (a) is false (\(n=4\) is even and not divisible by \(6\)), and so is the inverse (c) — the same \(n=4\) breaks it.

Common slips.

✗ ✓
Giving the inverse when asked for the contrapositive The contrapositive needs both operations. Negating alone is the inverse, and it is not equivalent.
“If \(n\) is not even” left as-is when a cleaner form exists Not wrong, but say “if \(n\) is odd” — the exam’s options are usually phrased naturally.

Working

TipConcepts — the three rearrangements

From \(A \Rightarrow B\) there are exactly three things you can do:

Name Operation Form Equivalent to the original?
Converse reverse \(B \Rightarrow A\) No
Inverse negate not \(A \Rightarrow\) not \(B\) No
Contrapositive negate and reverse not \(B \Rightarrow\) not \(A\) Yes, always

How to keep them straight.

  • The contrapositive is the only one with two operations — and it is the only one that is safe.
  • The converse and the inverse are contrapositives of each other, so they are equivalent to each other — and both are unrelated to the original. (That is why they are true or false together: in Question 1, both (a) and (c) came out false.)

A picture. Read \(A \Rightarrow B\) as “circle \(A\) sits inside circle \(B\)”.

  • Contrapositive: outside \(B\) ⟹ outside \(A\). Look at the picture — if all of \(A\) is inside \(B\), then anything outside \(B\) certainly missed \(A\). Same picture, read from outside in. That is why it is always equivalent.
  • Converse: inside \(B\) ⟹ inside \(A\). That would need the two circles to coincide, which is a genuinely different claim.

Question 2

Topic — why the contrapositive is equivalent, and the converse is not

Assume this statement is true:

If it rains, then the ground gets wet.

For each of the following, state whether it must be true, must be false, or cannot be determined.

  (a) If the ground is not wet, then it did not rain. [1]

  (b) If the ground is wet, then it rained. [1]

  (c) If it did not rain, then the ground is not wet. [1]

  (d) It rained and the ground is not wet. [1]

Identify each one before you judge it: (a), (b), (c) are the contrapositive, the converse and the inverse in some order. Only one of them is forced to be true. For (b) and (c), ask the honest question — could the ground be wet for some other reason? A burst pipe, a hose, a neighbour washing a car. Part (d) is different in kind: it is not a rearrangement at all, but a claim that the original statement is broken.

NoteSolution

Let \(A\) = “it rains”, \(B\) = “the ground gets wet”. Given: \(A \Rightarrow B\) is true.

(a) Must be true. This is the contrapositive (not \(B \Rightarrow\) not \(A\)). Dry ground rules out rain, because rain would have guaranteed wet ground. ✓

(b) Cannot be determined. This is the converse (\(B \Rightarrow A\)). Wet ground is consistent with a burst pipe. The original statement simply says nothing about it — it might be true in some other world, but it does not follow.

(c) Cannot be determined. This is the inverse (not \(A \Rightarrow\) not \(B\)). Same reason: no rain does not mean dry ground, because something else could wet it.

(d) Must be false. This says \(A\) is true and \(B\) is false — which is exactly the one combination \(A \Rightarrow B\) forbids. If it could happen, the original statement would be false.

\(\blacksquare\)

Notice that (b) and (c) received the same verdict. That is not a coincidence: the converse and the inverse are contrapositives of each other, so they always stand or fall together.

Common slips.

✗ ✓
(b) or (c) marked “must be false” They are not false — they are undetermined. Nothing in the statement settles them either way.
(d) treated as a rearrangement It is not an implication at all. It is the single case that would make the original false.

Working

TipConcepts — the one forbidden combination

\(A \Rightarrow B\) makes exactly one promise: you will never find \(A\) true while \(B\) is false. Everything else is permitted.

How to read the table. Each row is one possible situation — a combination of “is \(A\) true?” and “is \(B\) true?”. The last column asks: would that situation make the promise a lie?

Row \(A\) \(B\) Situation, in the rain example Promise broken?
1 true true it rained, ground wet no — exactly as promised
2 true false it rained, ground dry YES — the only way to break it
3 false true no rain, ground wet no — a burst pipe; the promise said nothing about this
4 false false no rain, ground dry no — again, nothing was promised

Rows 3 and 4 are why the converse and inverse cannot be deduced. When \(A\) is false, the statement makes no claim at all about \(B\) — so \(B\) is free to be either. Row 3 is precisely the counterexample that kills the converse.

Why this makes the contrapositive equivalent — slowly.

Two statements are the same statement when they are broken by exactly the same situations. So ask, for the contrapositive “not \(B \Rightarrow\) not \(A\)”, which row breaks it.

An implication is broken when its front is true and its back is false. For “not \(B \Rightarrow\) not \(A\)”, that means:

  • front true:   “not \(B\)” is true  \(\longrightarrow\)  \(B\) is false
  • back false:   “not \(A\)” is false  \(\longrightarrow\)  \(A\) is true

So the contrapositive is broken when \(A\) is true and \(B\) is false — row 2, the same single row that breaks the original. Same breaking condition, same statement. ∎

Now try the same test on the converse (\(B \Rightarrow A\)): it is broken when \(B\) is true and \(A\) is false — that is row 3, a different row. Different breaking condition, different statement. That is the whole distinction, in one calculation.

Proving something is false. To show \(A \Rightarrow B\) is false, you must produce a case where \(A\) holds and \(B\) fails — a counterexample. Part (d) is what a counterexample looks like: not “I disagree”, but a specific situation the statement forbids.

Question 3

Topic — judging a statement, its converse and its contrapositive

Let \(a\) and \(b\) be integers. Consider

If \(a\) and \(b\) are both odd, then \(ab\) is odd.

  (a) Is the statement true? [1]

  (b) Write its converse, and decide whether the converse is true. [2]

  (c) Write its contrapositive. Without doing any new work, say whether it is true and explain how you know. [2]

Part (c) is the point of the question, and it should cost you no effort at all: once you have settled (a), the contrapositive’s truth is already decided, because a statement and its contrapositive are the same statement. If you find yourself proving (c) from scratch, you have missed the shortcut this whole session is about.

NoteSolution

(a) True. If \(a = 2m+1\) and \(b = 2n+1\) then

\[ab = (2m+1)(2n+1) = 4mn + 2m + 2n + 1 = 2(2mn + m + n) + 1 ,\]

which is odd. ✓

(b) Converse: If \(ab\) is odd, then \(a\) and \(b\) are both odd.

This is also true. (If either factor were even, say \(a = 2k\), then \(ab = 2kb\) would be even. So an odd product forces both factors odd.)

(c) Contrapositive: If \(ab\) is not odd, then \(a\) and \(b\) are not both odd — more naturally, if \(ab\) is even, then at least one of \(a\), \(b\) is even.

It is true, and we know this without any work: it is the contrapositive of a true statement, and a statement and its contrapositive are always equivalent. Since (a) is true, so is (c). ✓

\(\blacksquare\)

Watch the negation in (c). The negation of “\(a\) and \(b\) are both odd” is not “both are even” — it is “at least one is even”. Negating an “and” flips it to an “or”: that is Session 4, but the trap is here already.

Common slips.

✗ ✓
Proving (c) from scratch Unnecessary. (a) true \(\Rightarrow\) (c) true, automatically. That is the whole value of the contrapositive.
(c) written as “if \(ab\) is even, then \(a\) and \(b\) are both even” Over-negated. “not (both odd)” means at least one is even, not both.
Concluding from (b) that converses are usually true Here both directions happen to hold — because “odd product” and “both factors odd” really are the same condition, so the pair forms an “if and only if”. That is unusual: compare Question 1, where the converse was false.

Working

TipConcepts — what you get for free, and what you must earn
You have proved You also get, free You still owe
\(A \Rightarrow B\) its contrapositive, not \(B \Rightarrow\) not \(A\) the converse \(B \Rightarrow A\)
\(A \Leftrightarrow B\) everything — all four forms nothing

So when a question gives you a true statement and asks about a rearrangement:

  1. Work out which rearrangement it is (converse / inverse / contrapositive).
  2. If it is the contrapositive — done, it is true, no work needed.
  3. If it is the converse or inverse — you must check it separately. It may be true, it may be false; the original tells you nothing.

When the converse also turns out true, as in part (b), the two statements are equivalent and you may write them as one “if and only if”. Many definitions are exactly this: “\(ab\) is odd if and only if \(a\) and \(b\) are both odd.”

Part 2 — Medium

Question 4

Topic — proof by contrapositive

Let \(n\) be an integer. Prove that

if \(n^2\) is even, then \(n\) is even.

  (a) Write down the contrapositive of the statement. [1]

  (b) Prove the contrapositive. [3]

  (c) Explain why this proves the original statement. [1]

Try proving the original directly and you will get stuck fast: “\(n^2\) is even” gives you \(n^2 = 2k\), and extracting information about \(n\) from that requires facts you have not been given. The contrapositive, though, starts from “\(n\) is odd” — and an odd number has a formula you can actually compute with, namely \(n = 2m+1\). That asymmetry is the whole reason this technique exists.

NoteSolution

(a) Contrapositive: If \(n\) is not even, then \(n^2\) is not even — that is,

if \(n\) is odd, then \(n^2\) is odd.

(b) Proof. Suppose \(n\) is odd. Then \(n = 2m+1\) for some integer \(m\), so

\[n^2 = (2m+1)^2 = 4m^2 + 4m + 1 = 2\left(2m^2 + 2m\right) + 1 .\]

Since \(2m^2 + 2m\) is an integer, \(n^2\) is odd. \(\blacksquare\)

(c) A statement and its contrapositive are logically equivalent — they are true in exactly the same circumstances. So having proved the contrapositive, the original statement is proved as well; there is nothing further to do.

Common slips.

✗ ✓
Proving the converse (“if \(n\) is even then \(n^2\) is even”) instead That is a different statement. True here, but it does not prove the original.
Writing \(n = 2m+1\) and then forgetting to say “for some integer \(m\)” The quantifier matters — it is what makes \(2m^2+2m\) an integer at the end.
Stopping at \(4m^2+4m+1\) Not finished. You must display it in the form \(2(\text{integer})+1\) to have shown it is odd.

Working

TipConcepts — when to reach for the contrapositive

The method.

  1. You want to prove \(A \Rightarrow B\) and the direct route is awkward.
  2. Write the contrapositive: not \(B \Rightarrow\) not \(A\).
  3. Prove that instead.
  4. Say the sentence: “since a statement and its contrapositive are equivalent, the original follows.”

The signal to use it. Reach for the contrapositive when the negations are easier to work with than the originals. Typical cases:

Original hypothesis Negation Which is easier?
\(n^2\) is even \(n\) is odd the negation — it gives you \(n = 2m+1\)
\(x\) is irrational \(x\) is rational the negation — it gives you \(x = p/q\)
“at least one of …” “none of …” the negation — one case instead of many

Anything of the form “if [something about a square/product] then [something about the number itself]” is a strong candidate, because squaring is easy and un-squaring is not.

Not the same as proof by contradiction. In a proof by contrapositive you assume not \(B\) and derive not \(A\) — a clean, direct argument. In a proof by contradiction you assume \(A\) and not \(B\) together and derive an absurdity. They feel similar and are often confused; contradiction is Session 5.

Question 5

Topic — contrapositives of statements written with “only if”

\(P\) and \(Q\) are statements. For each sentence below, write down its contrapositive, in the same style of English as the original.

  (a) \(P\) only if \(Q\). [2]

  (b) \(P\) if \(Q\). [2]

  (c) Hence explain why the contrapositive of “\(x>3\) only if \(x^2>9\)” is “\(x^2 \le 9\) only if \(x \le 3\)”. [2]

Do not try to negate an “only if” sentence directly — convert it to “if … then …” first, take the contrapositive there, and only then translate back if the question wants that style. Trying to shortcut this is how people produce the inverse by accident. Recall from S1: only if keeps the order, a bare if swaps it.

NoteSolution

(a) “\(P\) only if \(Q\)” means \(P \Rightarrow Q\).

Its contrapositive is \(\text{not } Q \Rightarrow \text{not } P\), i.e. if not \(Q\), then not \(P\). Written back in “only if” style:

not \(Q\) only if not \(P\).

(Both are correct answers; the official Notes give exactly this pairing.)

(b) “\(P\) if \(Q\)” means \(Q \Rightarrow P\) — the arrow starts at \(Q\).

Its contrapositive is \(\text{not } P \Rightarrow \text{not } Q\), i.e.

not \(P\) if not \(Q\)   (equivalently: if not \(P\), then not \(Q\)).

(c) Take \(P\) = “\(x>3\)” and \(Q\) = “\(x^2>9\)”. The given sentence is “\(P\) only if \(Q\)”, so by part (a) its contrapositive is “not \(Q\) only if not \(P\)”. Now negate each half:

  • not \(Q\) is “\(x^2 \le 9\)”   (the negation of \(>\) is \(\le\), not \(<\))
  • not \(P\) is “\(x \le 3\)”

giving exactly “\(x^2 \le 9\) only if \(x \le 3\)”. ✓

\(\blacksquare\)

Sanity check that it says something true: if \(x^2 \le 9\) then \(-3 \le x \le 3\), so certainly \(x \le 3\). ✓

Common slips.

✗ ✓
Negating \(x > 3\) to \(x < 3\) The negation of “greater than” is “less than or equal to”: \(x \le 3\). Forgetting the equality case is a standard TMUA trap.
Taking the contrapositive of (b) as “not \(Q\) only if not \(P\)” (b)’s arrow runs \(Q \Rightarrow P\), so its contrapositive negates in the other order: not \(P \Rightarrow\) not \(Q\).

Working

TipConcepts — the routine, and negating comparisons

The routine for any awkward wording:

\[\text{English sentence} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{“if … then …”} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{contrapositive} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{(translate back if asked)}\]

Never operate on the original English directly.

The official Notes’ table, worth recognising:

Statement Contrapositive
if \(A\) then \(B\) if not \(B\) then not \(A\)
\(A\) only if \(B\) not \(B\) only if not \(A\)

Negating comparisons — get these exactly right, because half-marks live here:

Statement Its negation
\(x > 3\) \(x \le 3\)
\(x \ge 3\) \(x < 3\)
\(x = 3\) \(x \ne 3\)
\(1 < x < 4\) \(x \le 1\) or \(x \ge 4\)

The pattern: negating a strict inequality gives a non-strict one, and vice versa — the boundary case always changes sides.

Part 3 — Hard

Question 6

Topic — sorting several rephrasings at exam speed

\(m\) is a positive integer. Consider the theorem

\[\text{If } m^2 \text{ is divisible by } 4, \text{ then } m \text{ is even.} \tag{$*$}\]

Which of the following, taken individually, is/are equivalent to \((*)\)?

  I   If \(m\) is odd, then \(m^2\) is not divisible by \(4\).

  II   If \(m\) is even, then \(m^2\) is divisible by \(4\).

  III   \(m^2\) is divisible by \(4\) only if \(m\) is even.

  A I only    B II only    C III only    D I and II    E I and III    F II and III    G I, II and III    H None

Name the halves and write \((*)\) as an arrow before you read the options — ten seconds spent there saves a minute of confusion. Then classify each of I, II, III as original / converse / inverse / contrapositive. Be warned that all three statements happen to be true, so truth will not discriminate between them; the question is which ones say the same thing as \((*)\).

NoteSolution

Let \(P\) = “\(m^2\) is divisible by \(4\)” and \(Q\) = “\(m\) is even”. Then \((*) = P \Rightarrow Q\).

I. “If \(m\) is odd, then \(m^2\) is not divisible by \(4\)” is \(\text{not } Q \Rightarrow \text{not } P\) — negated and reversed. That is the contrapositive. Equivalent. ✓

II. “If \(m\) is even, then \(m^2\) is divisible by \(4\)” is \(Q \Rightarrow P\) — reversed only. That is the converse. Not equivalent — even though, as it happens, it is a true statement. ✗

III. “\(P\) only if \(Q\)” is \(P \Rightarrow Q\) — which is \((*)\) itself, word for word. Equivalent. ✓

Answer: E (I and III). \(\blacksquare\)

Why II is the trap. It is true, it is about the same two facts, and it feels like a restatement. But \(Q \Rightarrow P\) is the converse, and a converse is a different claim even when it is a true one. The question asks “equivalent to”, not “also true”.

Common slips.

✗ ✓
Including II because it is true Truth is not equivalence. Compare the arrows, not the truth values.
Rejecting I because it “looks different” It is negated and reversed — the definition of the contrapositive, always equivalent.
Missing III because “only if” was unfamiliar “\(P\) only if \(Q\)” is just \(P \Rightarrow Q\) — the original, restyled.

Working

TipConcepts — a 30-second exam routine
  1. Name the halves. Write “\(P\) = …, \(Q\) = …” on your paper.
  2. Write the theorem as one arrow, \(P \Rightarrow Q\).
  3. Convert each option to an arrow, using the S1 rules for if / only if.
  4. Match by shape, not by meaning:
What you see It is Equivalent?
\(P \Rightarrow Q\) the original (perhaps restyled) ✓
not \(Q \Rightarrow\) not \(P\) contrapositive ✓
\(Q \Rightarrow P\) converse ✗
not \(P \Rightarrow\) not \(Q\) inverse ✗

Never let the truth of a statement enter this decision. Exam writers deliberately choose theorems where the converse is also true (as here), precisely so that candidates who reason “it’s true, so it must be equivalent” get it wrong.

Question 7

Topic — spotting a converse used as though it were a contrapositive

A student is asked to prove:

If \(n^3\) is odd, then \(n\) is odd.

The student writes:

“Suppose \(n\) is odd. Then \(n = 2k+1\), so \[n^3 = (2k+1)^3 = 8k^3 + 12k^2 + 6k + 1 = 2\left(4k^3 + 6k^2 + 3k\right) + 1 ,\] which is odd. Hence if \(n^3\) is odd, then \(n\) is odd.”

  (a) Is the student’s algebra correct? [1]

  (b) Is the student’s proof correct? Explain precisely what has been proved and what was required. [3]

  (c) Write a correct proof of the original statement. [2]

Read the student’s first line and the last line, and ask what was assumed versus what was required. The algebra can be flawless while the logic is wrong — that combination is exactly what Paper 2’s “find the error” questions are built from, and it is far harder to spot than an arithmetic slip.

NoteSolution

(a) Yes, the algebra is correct. \((2k+1)^3 = 8k^3 + 12k^2 + 6k + 1\), and that equals \(2(4k^3+6k^2+3k) + 1\), which is odd. Nothing is wrong with any line of it.

(b) No, the proof is not correct. Look at what was assumed and what was concluded:

  • The student assumed \(n\) is odd and derived \(n^3\) is odd. That proves \[(n \text{ odd}) \Rightarrow (n^3 \text{ odd}) .\]
  • The question asked for \[(n^3 \text{ odd}) \Rightarrow (n \text{ odd}) .\]

So the student has proved the converse of what was required. The converse is not equivalent to the original, so nothing about the original has been established — the final sentence (“Hence …”) does not follow.

It is worth noting that the statement the student proved is perfectly true, and so is the one they were asked for. But proving one does not prove the other, and that is the error.

(c) A correct proof. Use the contrapositive: if \(n\) is even, then \(n^3\) is even.

Suppose \(n\) is even, so \(n = 2k\) for some integer \(k\). Then

\[n^3 = (2k)^3 = 8k^3 = 2\left(4k^3\right) ,\]

which is even. Since a statement and its contrapositive are equivalent, the original — if \(n^3\) is odd then \(n\) is odd — is proved. \(\blacksquare\)

The near-miss. The student’s work is only a small edit away from being right: had they started from “\(n\) is even” instead of “\(n\) is odd”, they would have had exactly the proof in (c). Choosing which statement to assume is the entire skill here.

Common slips.

✗ ✓
“The proof is fine because the algebra checks out” Correct algebra proving the wrong statement is still a failed proof. Always compare assumed with required.
Fixing it by “reversing the steps” The steps are not reversible as written. Restart from the contrapositive, as in (c).
Calling the student’s error a contrapositive They proved the converse. Their argument contains no negations at all — the giveaway.

Working

TipConcepts — auditing any proof in two lines

Whenever you are asked whether a proof is valid, ignore the algebra at first and write down two things:

\[\textbf{ASSUMED: } \underline{\qquad\qquad} \qquad\qquad \textbf{REQUIRED: } \underline{\qquad\qquad}\]

Then check the match:

Assumed → concluded Verdict
exactly what was required ✓ valid (direct proof)
not \(B\) → not \(A\), when \(A \Rightarrow B\) was required ✓ valid (proof by contrapositive)
\(B\) → \(A\), when \(A \Rightarrow B\) was required ✗ proved the converse
not \(A\) → not \(B\), when \(A \Rightarrow B\) was required ✗ proved the inverse

A quick tell: a genuine proof by contrapositive contains negations — it starts from “suppose \(n\) is even”, “suppose \(x\) is rational”. If a student claims contrapositive but their argument has no negation anywhere, they have almost certainly proved the converse instead.
